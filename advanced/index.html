Where is the calculus?
  • The force is a derivative. F = −dU/dx: minus the slope of the energy curve. Steep curve, strong push, and always downhill.
  • Newton's law is a differential equation. Acceleration is the second derivative of position, so F = ma reads m·x″ = −dU/dx. For real molecules it cannot be solved by hand, so the computer takes tiny steps in time.
  • The Verlet step is two Taylor series. Expand x(t + dt) and x(t − dt) around t and add them: the odd terms (dt, dt3) cancel, leaving x(t + dt) = 2x(t) − x(t − dt) + a(t)·dt2 with an error of order dt4.
  • Near a minimum every curve is a parabola. At a minimum U′(x0) = 0, so the Taylor series starts with ½U″(x0)(x − x0)2. That is Hooke's law: a chemical bond near its rest length behaves like a spring.
  • Rare events follow an exponential. The crossing rate is proportional to e−h/T, the Arrhenius equation. The barrier sits in the exponent, so a little more barrier means a lot fewer crossings.

Where is the calculus?
  • A sum of Gaussians rebuilds the curve. Each hill is w·exp(−(x − xi)2/(2σ2)). Their sum V(x) fills the valley the atom is stuck in, and once the run is long enough −V(x) is a copy of U(x).
  • The extra push is a derivative. The atom feels F = −dU/dx − dV/dx, and dV/dx is the sum of the derivatives of the Gaussians, one term at a time.
  • Why the trick is needed. Without hills the crossing rate is proportional to e−h/T, the Arrhenius equation, so at low T the atom would wait practically forever. Filling the valley lowers the barrier, and the waiting time shrinks exponentially.
  • Same force, same law. The atom still obeys m·x″ = F and still moves by the Verlet step; only the energy curve it rolls on has changed.
Reduced units: m = 1, kB = 1. One particle on a one-dimensional energy curve, moved by the Verlet step.
